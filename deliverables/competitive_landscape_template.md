# Part 1. Porter Five Forces

## Threat of new entrants
- Barriers to entry: 
A major barrier to entry for prediction market platforms in Georgia is the National Bank of Georgia's (NBG) 250,000 GEL (~$92,000 USD) minimum capital requirement for Payment Service Provider (PSP) registration, mandated by the "Law on Payment Systems and Payment Services" [NBG PSP Regulation](https://legal.ge/en/service/banking-and-finance-law-en/fintech-licensing-en). The field is also regulated by the "Law on Microfinance Organizations" and NBG's VASP Decree requiring identical capital plus Travel Rule transaction monitoring systems.
Historically, Georgian fintechs like Payze ($750K seed), TakePay ($1.2M), and CityPay.io ($2.5M) raised 2-10x the regulatory minimum to cover technology (oracles/trading engines), team, and liquidity seeding, averaging $1.5M seed rounds for PSP-licensed platforms. Realistic Year 1 capital need reaches 2.1M GEL ($775K USD), creating substantial barriers despite Georgia's low regulatory threshold.

- Sources of advantage for current leaders: TBC Capital's brokerage arm doubled its customer base to 100,000+ in 2025 and grew AUM from $735M to $850M — it already owns the distribution channel through the ubiquitous TBC app. Investing.com Galt & Taggart, the investment-banking arm of the LSE-listed Bank of Georgia Group (BGEO), holds the 2025 Euromoney "Best Broker in Georgia" title and deep institutional relationships. Euromoney These incumbents benefit from bundled financial relationships, users who already have their payroll, savings, mortgage, and investment account with one entity face a high perceived switching cost even if the product itself is free to leave.
- Likely new entrants: The 24 registered VASPs (crypto asset service providers) represent the most crypto-native adjacent threat. International platforms — Polymarket has expanded globally, and Kalshi is actively pursuing international partnerships — could seek Georgian regulatory approval as a pilot CIS/Caucasus market. Regional fintech accelerators in Armenia and Azerbaijan could produce cross-border players. Crucially, the NBG regulatory sandbox (established 2020) was specifically designed for exactly this category of innovative product and has already piloted equity crowdfunding models and tokenized deposits, making it the natural first port of call for a prediction market applicant.
- Force rating: Medium
Regulatory accessibility (especially the sandbox) keeps the door open. The dominant threat is not legal barriers but distribution dominance by the two banking groups. A focused, well-capitalized entrant with a differentiated product can overcome this — but execution margin for error is thin. 
- Strategic implication: Engage the NBG sandbox proactively before a competitor does. The sandbox grants regulatory breathing room for 12–24 months with reduced compliance overhead. Filing first creates a de-facto first-mover status in a jurisdiction with no playbook for this product category.

## Bargaining power of buyers
- Who are the main buyers? Retail users, skewing toward urban, digitally active Georgians aged 18–35. The Findex 2021 data shows 70.5% of Georgian adults hold transaction accounts and 62% have made at least one digital payment — a high baseline for a $6,086 per-capita-GDP country. Yet only 5.6% of household financial assets flow into securities, revealing a vast and latent pool of savers who have never been offered a compelling active-participation product. [The Finech Times](https://thefintechtimes.com/richie-not-done-yet-fintech-in-the-caucasus-armenia-azerbaijan-georgia/)
- How concentrated are they? Extremely fragmented. Georgia's population is 3.8 million; the addressable urban digitally-banked audience is perhaps 800,000–1.2 million. No single user segment is concentrated enough to exert individual pricing power. The risk is collective behavior: users vote with engagement and churn is digital, silent, and near-instantaneous.
- How easy is switching? Trivially easy — every product in this space is a free-to-download mobile app. Switching costs are effectively zero beyond sunk emotional investment in an active position. Behavioral stickiness must be engineered through product design (streaks, leaderboards, social features) rather than lock-in contractual mechanisms.
- Force rating: High
Users hold all the UX leverage. Any friction in onboarding, deposit/withdrawal, or contract resolution will be immediately punished with churn and negative word-of-mouth.

## Bargaining power of suppliers
- Who are the critical suppliers? Payment rail providers — TBC Bank and Bank of Georgia are the duopoly. Any fintech needing GEL deposits/withdrawals must negotiate with one or both. The 35 licensed PSPs provide some alternative routing, but ultimately settlement with Georgian banks is unavoidable. This is the single highest-leverage supplier relationship in the business model. Notably, Georgian law requires payment service providers to block user transactions to unlicensed gambling sites, meaning banking partners will scrutinize the platform's licensing status before onboarding it.
- How concentrated are they? Payment rails: Highly concentrated, low replaceability in the short run (crypto rails are a partial escape valve via the 24 VASPs, but GEL liquidity remains bank-controlled)
- How replaceable are they? While open banking and APIs reduce some reliance on incumbent banks, fintech firms cannot completely replace core banking infrastructure suppliers:Opening accounts still needs a licensed bank partner.Real‑time settlement and compliance require banking rails.

- Force rating: High
The payment rail concentration is the critical risk. If either major bank declines to serve the platform — or uses its merchant agreement to block user transfers — the business model collapses. This is the Georgian equivalent of the "bank de-risking" problem that has strangled multiple fintech categories globally.
- Strategic implication: Structure the payment architecture to maximize optionality from day one. Seek PSP licenses independently. Explore crypto (USDC) settlement as a backup or partial primary rail. Negotiate banking partnerships as a strategic priority, potentially offering the banks a data/co-branding value proposition to make them partners rather than gatekeepers.

## Competitive rivalry
- Number and type of competitors: Georgia's fintech competitive landscape is defined by a concentrated banking duopoly, a fragmented fintech startup ecosystem, and intense but asymmetric competition across different market segments. The sector is dominated by two systemic banks — TBC Bank and Bank of Georgia — which together control approximately 70-74% of banking assets and loans through their combined market shares of roughly 37% each. This creates a highly concentrated core where these incumbents compete fiercely for premium retail and corporate clients while maintaining largely parallel pricing strategies. Beyond this duopoly, approximately 50 active fintech firms operate in Georgia as of 2022, ranging from payment processors and digital lenders to neobanks and blockchain startups . These smaller players typically avoid direct confrontation with the banking giants, instead carving out niches in underserved segments such as micro-lending for gig economy workers, cross-border remittances, or merchant payment solutions for small retailers.
- Basis of competition: The basis of competition varies significantly across these tiers. For TBC and Bank of Georgia, rivalry centers on digital experience differentiation, brand trust, and corporate banking relationships rather than price. Both banks have launched sophisticated digital banking platforms — TBC's Space and Bank of Georgia's Solo — which compete aggressively on user interface design, speed of onboarding, and ecosystem integration with e-commerce and lifestyle services. In contrast, the smaller fintech cohort competes primarily on accessibility and speed, targeting customers who are either unbanked or frustrated by the legacy processes of major banks. This includes faster loan approvals, simplified KYC procedures, and specialized products like buy-now-pay-later services or crypto-fiat bridges that the large banks either cannot or choose not to offer due to regulatory caution.
- Price intensity: Price intensity in the Georgian market is moderate but selectively fierce. In traditional banking products such as deposits and mortgages, pricing remains remarkably stable due to the implicit coordination between the two dominant players, with interest rate movements closely mirroring National Bank of Georgia policy shifts rather than competitive undercutting. However, in payment processing and micro-lending, price competition intensifies significantly as fintech startups undercut bank fees to gain market share, often operating on thinner margins sustained by venture capital backing or cross-subsidization from other business lines. The payments market itself has grown from just under $1 billion in 2022 to $1.18 billion in 2023, with projections reaching $2.2 billion by 2027, attracting new entrants willing to compete aggressively on transaction fees and merchant acquisition cost
- Innovation intensity: Innovation intensity is high and accelerating, driven by regulatory modernization and demographic tailwinds. The National Bank of Georgia's implementation of open banking frameworks in 2023, combined with sandbox licensing for fintech experiments, has lowered barriers to entry and encouraged rapid product iteration . Both major banks have responded by significantly increasing their technology investments, with TBC Bank specifically highlighting digital transformation and AI-driven customer analytics as strategic priorities in its 2024 investor communications . Meanwhile, the startup ecosystem innovates around the edges, particularly in areas like embedded finance, blockchain-based settlement, and mobile-first insurance products where regulatory ambiguity or legacy infrastructure constraints have historically limited incumbent agility.
- Force rating: Given these dynamics, the overall force rating for competitive rivalry is Medium-High. While the market avoids the pure price destruction seen in fully commoditized sectors, the combination of duopolistic intensity at the top, disruptive pressure from below, and rapid technological change creates a challenging environment where sustained competitive advantage requires continuous investment in digital capabilities and customer experience. The 70% asset concentration provides stability but also invites regulatory scrutiny and fintech disruption, while the fragmented nature of the remaining 30% ensures persistent competitive pressure from agile challengers.
- Strategic implication: For new entrants, success requires either extreme specialization in segments the major banks ignore or a partnership strategy that leverages incumbent infrastructure while delivering superior UX. For the dominant banks, the imperative is to maintain innovation velocity despite scale advantages, as the 24 registered VASPs and growing fintech cohort demonstrate that regulatory compliance and brand trust — while powerful — are no longer sufficient to prevent customer defection among digitally native demographics. Both TBC and Bank of Georgia must balance defensive protection of their core deposit and lending franchises with offensive investments in platform ecosystems that embed financial services into broader digital lifestyles, thereby raising switching costs and blurring the lines between banking and non-banking financial services.

### Value chain interpretation
- Which stage appears most vulnerable? Customer Acquisition & KYC
This stage exhibits the clearest vulnerability due to persistent friction and emerging competitive threats. Despite the National Bank of Georgia's push toward digitalization, onboarding remains comparatively slow with 24-48 hour KYC timelines versus near-instant verification in mature fintech markets. The 24 registered VASPs have demonstrated that younger, digitally native customers will abandon traditional channels for faster alternatives, even when those alternatives carry higher risk profiles. Furthermore, the fragmentation of customer data across approximately 50 fintech firms and multiple banking groups prevents the development of unified identity layers that could reduce acquisition costs industry-wide. As open banking matures, fintechs with superior UX and telco partnerships are positioned to poach customers from incumbents at the top of the funnel, making this stage both high-cost and high-churn.
- Which stage appears most defensible? Core Banking & Payments Infrastructure
This stage remains strongly fortified by structural and regulatory barriers. TBC Bank and Bank of Georgia's combined 70-74% market share in assets and loans reflects decades of trust accumulation, regulatory relationships, and physical presence that cannot be rapidly replicated . The National Bank of Georgia's licensing requirements and capital adequacy rules create meaningful entry barriers, while the payments infrastructure — though growing rapidly toward $2.2 billion by 2027 — remains anchored to traditional banking rails for final settlement. Even the 24 VASPs ultimately depend on banking partnerships for fiat on-ramps and off-ramps. Profit margins in lending remain attractive at 6-8% net interest margins, providing incumbents with resources to defend their position through technology investment . The 2023 open banking regulations, while theoretically disruptive, have been implemented gradually enough to allow major banks to adapt without losing structural advantage .
- Where could a platform insert itself?
A strategic platform could insert itself most effectively at the intersection of customer acquisition and wealth management, specifically by bridging the gap between speculative crypto participation and regulated financial services. The verified absence of prediction market platforms combined with the demonstrated appetite for VASP-style speculation among Georgia's young, urban demographic suggests an opportunity for a regulated alternative that offers similar UX with traditional financial safeguards. Such a platform would leverage the digital pressure points in KYC (speed, biometrics, data integration) and the vulnerability of wealth management (underserved retail investors, absence of robo-advisors) while partnering with rather than replacing core banking infrastructure. Additionally, embedded finance partnerships with Georgia's strong telecom sector (Silknet, Magti) or ride-hailing platforms (Bolt, which operates extensively in Tbilisi) represent viable insertion points at the distribution stage, bypassing the high CAC of direct customer acquisition while tapping into existing digital ecosystems.

# Part 3. Jobs-to-be-done competition

## Core customer job
What job is the customer trying to get done?
The fundamental job that Georgian customers — particularly the young, urban, digitally native demographic driving fintech adoption — are trying to accomplish is "make my money work harder and faster without requiring me to become a financial expert or wait for permission." This job transcends traditional banking categories. It encompasses immediate needs like paying friends instantly, speculative needs like turning small amounts of money into meaningful returns through accessible instruments, and longer-term needs like building financial security without navigating intimidating institutional processes. The customer is not hiring a bank account, a trading platform, or a payment app; they are hiring progress toward financial agency in a country where traditional structures have historically favored those with existing wealth, connections, or patience for bureaucratic complexity. The approximately 50 fintech firms and 24 VASPs operating in Georgia succeed precisely to the extent that they remove friction from this progress, while the dominance of TBC and Bank of Georgia reflects their success at providing trust-based alternatives for customers prioritizing security over speed

## Functional needs
Customers need to move money instantly at any hour without branch visits or paperwork delays. They need to start participating in financial markets with amounts as small as ten or twenty lari, not the hundreds or thousands traditional wealth management requires. They need transparent, predictable costs without hidden fees that erode small transactions disproportionately. They need their financial tools to integrate seamlessly with daily life — paying for rides, splitting restaurant bills, topping up mobile credit, sending remittances to family abroad — without switching between multiple apps or interfaces. They need credit decisions based on actual behavior and cash flow rather than employment certificates or collateral they do not possess. They need protection from fraud and volatility without sacrificing the autonomy that attracted them to digital alternatives in the first place. Finally, they need financial progress to feel visible and rewarding through dashboards, notifications, and milestones that make abstract money concrete and motivating.
## Emotional or social needs
Customers need to feel financially competent and modern rather than ignorant or left behind. In Georgia's rapidly urbanizing society with 82% urban population and strong generational mobility, financial tools serve as signals of social status and forward-looking identity . They need to participate in conversations and communities around money, investments, and economic trends without embarrassment or exclusion. They need reassurance that their financial choices are legitimate and protected, countering the anxiety that digital platforms might be schemes or that informal alternatives might evaporate overnight. They need to demonstrate financial capability to family members, peers, and potential partners — particularly important in a culture where family financial interdependence remains strong. They crave the dopamine of small wins and visible progress, the emotional reward that speculative platforms deliver through price movements and gamified interfaces. They also need freedom from the shame of financial exclusion, whether that means avoiding the embarrassment of declined transactions, the indignity of rejected loan applications, or the dependency of asking relatives for emergency funds.

## Alternative solutions
Direct alternatives include the 24 registered VASPs offering crypto trading with instant gratification and community belonging, though with regulatory ambiguity and volatility risk . The major banks — TBC through Space and Bank of Georgia through Solo — provide direct competition with increasingly sophisticated digital experiences that trade some speed for trust and regulatory protection . International platforms like PayPal, Wise, and Revolut capture cross-border flows and FX needs, particularly among the Georgian diaspora and import-export entrepreneurs. Smaller fintech lenders offer faster credit decisions than banks, albeit at higher rates. Informal rotating savings associations persist in rural areas and among older demographics, offering social cohesion alongside financial returns.

Indirect alternatives are equally significant and often underestimated. Real estate speculation — purchasing apartments in Tbilisi or Batumi for rental or resale — absorbs substantial household capital that might otherwise enter formal financial markets, offering tangible assets and cultural prestige. Cash hoarding under mattresses or in safes remains prevalent for small amounts, driven by privacy concerns and distrust of digital systems. Consumption itself serves as an alternative to financial participation, with discretionary spending on dining, travel, and electronics delivering immediate emotional returns that deferred financial goals cannot match. Employment choices represent another indirect alternative, with many Georgians prioritizing jobs offering immediate cash payments or side-hustle opportunities over formal employment with banking benefits. Finally, emigration functions as a radical alternative, with significant portions of working-age Georgians seeking economic opportunity abroad rather than optimizing their domestic financial lives.

## Interpretation
This jobs-to-be-done analysis reveals that Georgian fintechs and banks are not merely competing against each other; they are competing against time, attention, trust deficits, and alternative pathways to financial and emotional fulfillment that sit entirely outside the financial sector. The most dangerous competitors are not the 50 fintech firms or the banking duopoly, but rather the VASPs that satisfy the emotional need for participation and progress without requiring financial literacy, the real estate market that offers tangible status and returns, and consumption culture that delivers immediate gratification.
The hidden competition exposes a critical strategic insight: speed and accessibility matter more than product sophistication. Customers will tolerate volatility, regulatory ambiguity, and limited functionality if a solution makes them feel competent, included, and progressing. TBC and Bank of Georgia defend their position through trust and ecosystem breadth, but their vulnerability lies in the emotional lag between their polished apps and the visceral excitement customers experience on speculative platforms. Conversely, fintech startups and VASPs win on emotional engagement but lose on security and longevity concerns.
The analysis also suggests that financial education as traditionally conceived is insufficient competition. Customers do not want to become experts; they want to hire outcomes. Platforms that disguise complexity behind simple interfaces — whether through automated investing, goal-based savings, or social features that make financial behavior contagious — will outcompete those requiring active customer learning and management.
Finally, the prevalence of indirect alternatives indicates that the addressable market for formal fintech is smaller than raw demographic data suggests. A significant portion of potential customers are effectively lost to real estate, cash, consumption, or emigration. The strategic imperative is not universal conversion but rather identifying and dominating the segments — young urban professionals, gig economy workers, diaspora-connected households — where the core job of financial progress without friction resonates most intensely and alternative solutions remain unsatisfying.
