# Framework: Transformation Failure Patterns

## Source
Christensen, Clayton, *The Innovator's Dilemma*
Rogers, *The Digital Transformation Roadmap*
Case analysis: Blockbuster, Kodak, and NHS Digital

---

## Why transformations fail

The most common misdiagnosis: transformation failure is attributed to technology. The actual causes are strategic, financial, and organisational.

Both Blockbuster and Kodak had access to the technology. Both failed for reasons that had nothing to do with technical capability.

---

## Case 1: Blockbuster

### The numbers
- Late fee revenue: $800 million per year, 16 percent of total revenue.
- Netflix offered to sell to Blockbuster in 2000 for $50 million. Blockbuster declined.
- 2002: 60 million customers, $6 billion revenue, 9,000 stores.
- 2010: filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Netflix market capitalisation at the same date: $3 billion.

### The timeline of a rational failure

| Year | Decision | Rationale | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2000 | Board declines Netflix acquisition offer at $50M | Late fee revenue is $800M per year and cannot be replaced by streaming revenue in the near term | The only realistic acquisition opportunity is closed |
| 2002 | Strategy: defend and extend the retail model | Peak dominance; no immediate competitive threat | Capital directed toward store expansion rather than digital infrastructure |
| 2004 | Launches Blockbuster Online | Four years behind Netflix; Netflix has 3 million subscribers | Enters streaming at a structural disadvantage with a legacy cost base |
| 2007 | Internal review: dropping late fees requires finding $800M elsewhere in one year | The board cannot approve a transformation that destroys the most profitable line | The business model constraint becomes terminal |
| 2010 | Chapter 11 bankruptcy | Asset-heavy model cannot pivot without liquidating the core business | Complete market position reversal in 10 years |

### Three failure mechanisms

1. Business model protection: incumbents optimise for today's profit. Any initiative threatening the most profitable revenue stream is rejected by the P&L, even when the long-run case is clear.

2. Channel conflict: digital moves threaten existing partner and distributor relationships. Blockbuster's retail partners benefited from in-store traffic. Digital bypassed them. The partners pushed back.

3. Capability mismatch: the skills that made Blockbuster successful (real estate, retail operations, logistics) were irrelevant to streaming. The very assets that created dominance became liabilities.

### The lesson for your final project

Every organisation has its equivalent of late fee revenue. The board-level constraint in your client's industry is the business model element that makes transformation hardest to approve. Name it explicitly in your executive summary. Show that the cost of inaction exceeds the short-term revenue impact. Make the board choose between two risks, not between change and comfort.

## Case 2: Kodak

### The paradox

Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975. It filed for bankruptcy in 2012. The invention was never the problem.

### The invention trap

Kodak's digital camera was suppressed because film revenue was too important. R&D investment continued internally but no product was launched. By 1991, when Kodak finally launched a commercial digital camera, Canon and Sony had established market positions. Kodak had the technology for 16 years and still lost.

Lesson: R&D without an operating model to commercialise the invention is not a competitive asset.

### The capability gap

Kodak's competency was chemistry, optics, and photo processing logistics. Its sales organisation was built around photo retailers. When the transition came, Kodak had no digital distribution, no software engineering competency, and no direct consumer relationship. The assets that built the empire were irrelevant to the successor market.

### What Three Horizons required

Kodak needed to:
1. Run digital as a separate H3 business unit from 1990, ring-fenced from the film P&L and not subject to film-era ROI hurdles.
2. Use H1 film profits to fund H2 digital infrastructure throughout the 1990s.
3. Accept that H1 (film) would decline. The strategic task was to fund H3 from H1 cash flows before H1 collapsed.

None of these decisions were made. The Three Horizons logic was not applied.

## Four failure patterns that kill final projects

These are the most common ways that strong analysis produces a weak final project. Recognise them and eliminate them in the sprint.

### Pattern 1: Technology pilot never scales to production

Root cause: data quality, architecture, or change management prerequisites were not met before deployment.

What it looks like in a final project: the roadmap shows AI deployment in month 4 but the data governance prerequisite takes 12 months. The financial model assumes deployment is successful at the projected date. The sequence is wrong and the numbers are wrong.

Fix: in the roadmap, every infrastructure prerequisite must appear before the AI deployment initiative it enables. State each prerequisite explicitly with a timeline and owner.

### Pattern 2: Protecting legacy revenue prevents investment

Root cause: the board approves only initiatives that do not threaten existing revenue. The transformation never gets approved.

What it looks like in a final project: the recommendation does not acknowledge the business model constraint. The financial case does not show the cost of inaction.

Fix: name the legacy revenue at risk in the executive summary. Show that the cost of inaction exceeds the short-term revenue loss. This is the Blockbuster argument made explicit.

### Pattern 3: New initiative run inside the existing P&L

Root cause: the new business unit is subject to the same ROI hurdles as the existing core business. H2 and H3 initiatives are killed by H1 performance requirements.

What it looks like in a final project: the recommendation uses the same hurdle rate and payback horizon for H1 efficiency initiatives and H3 platform experiments.

Fix: recommend a ring-fenced budget structure for H2 and H3 initiatives with explicitly different return timeline expectations than the H1 core business.

### Pattern 4: Roadmap is a list with dates, not a plan

Root cause: initiative list without dependencies, owners, or resource requirements. The roadmap cannot be implemented.

What it looks like in a final project: a table with initiative names and months but no dependencies, no owner functions, and no budget estimates. No board approves this.

Fix: every roadmap initiative must have all seven required elements. The seven elements are: name, horizon tag, timeline, owner function, budget estimate, dependency on a prior initiative, and one measurable success metric.

## Application to Weekend 3

Use these failure patterns as a diagnostic tool on your own final project:
- Which of your roadmap initiatives is most at risk of Pattern 1?
- What is your client's equivalent of Blockbuster's late fee revenue (Pattern 2)?
- Does your financial model use the same hurdle rate for H1 and H3 initiatives (Pattern 3)?
- Count the elements in your roadmap initiatives. If any are missing, the roadmap is incomplete (Pattern 4).
