# Industry and Competitive Analysis

## Project

Team A: Georgian Prediction-Market Platform  
Industry: Fintech  
Market: Republic of Georgia  
Client assumption: TBC-backed launch model  
Recommended strategic option: TBC-owned, GEL-settled prediction-market platform under an NBG special-designation or sandbox-first route

---

## Purpose

This document builds the industry and competitive context for the final project. It updates the Week 2 analysis by shifting the focus from a standalone Georgian fintech venture to a TBC-backed platform strategy.

The analysis answers four questions:

1. How do Rogers’ five domains apply to the Georgian prediction-market opportunity?
2. What is the correct Christensen disruption classification?
3. How attractive is the industry under Porter Five Forces?
4. What are the key competitive dynamics and their strategic consequences for TBC?

The central conclusion is:

> The opportunity is not simply to launch a new trading interface. The opportunity is for TBC to own the regulated wallet, customer relationship, market infrastructure, and governance layer for Georgian event-contract markets before foreign platforms, crypto wallets, betting operators, or bank competitors define the category.

---

## 1. Industry Definition

### Industry boundary

The relevant industry is not traditional banking alone and not crypto alone. The relevant industry is:

> Georgian retail fintech at the intersection of mobile banking, digital payments, investment access, crypto/VASP activity, betting substitutes, and emerging prediction markets.

The proposed product is a regulated event-contract marketplace where users take positions on clear, official-source outcomes such as inflation releases, GEL exchange-rate ranges, NBG policy decisions, election turnout, or other verifiable public-data events.

### Included in scope

- Mobile banking and digital wallet infrastructure
- Bank-backed retail fintech
- Brokerage and investment platforms
- Payment gateways and GEL settlement rails
- VASP and crypto platforms
- Betting and gambling substitutes as behavioral competition
- Global prediction-market benchmarks such as Polymarket, Kalshi, and Manifold
- NBG special-designation or sandbox-style pathway
- Responsible-use and consumer-protection controls

### Excluded from scope

- Unregulated offshore prediction-market launch
- Gambling-license strategy
- Sports-betting-first strategy
- Crypto-native launch as the base case
- Broad bank AI use cases unrelated to the platform
- Traditional corporate banking, lending, or wealth management outside the platform logic

### Strategic implication

The industry should be analyzed as a platform opportunity, not as a normal app launch. TBC’s advantage does not come from building the simplest interface. It comes from owning the wallet, KYC, GEL settlement, customer relationship, mobile app distribution, official-source resolution, and governance layer.

## 2. Market Context

Georgia already has the behavioral and infrastructure foundations for a prediction-market product.

The Week 2 industry analysis identified several relevant foundations:

- digital payments and mobile commerce are growing;
- mobile-first financial behavior is becoming normal;
- open banking and payment modernization are progressing;
- VASP registration has created a clearer digital-asset framework;
- TBC and Bank of Georgia already operate trusted digital banking ecosystems;
- brokerage access exists, but remains a different customer job from event-based trading.

The updated ROI model adds a stronger demand-side view. It uses a risk-active market proxy of **651,000 users**, based on gambling-related users, cryptocurrency users, and retail stock users. It also uses **GEL 6,546 average monthly turnover per active risk user** as the core turnover-velocity assumption. This does not mean users deposit or lose GEL 6,546 per month. It means capital cycles repeatedly through risk-based activity.

### Strategic implication

The attractive market gap is not “cheap trading.” Low-cost brokerage access already exists. The attractive gap is a regulated product for users who want simple, short-term, event-based exposure inside a trusted local financial ecosystem.

## 3. Rogers’ Five Domains Applied to Georgian Prediction Markets

Rogers’ five domains help explain why prediction markets are not just a new product category. They change customers, competition, data, innovation, and value.

### 3.1 Customers

#### Finding

Customers are shifting from passive financial users to active digital participants. They expect mobile-first access, transparent pricing, fast onboarding, and simple interfaces. Existing behavior in betting, crypto, and stock trading shows that part of the Georgian market already seeks high-frequency risk exposure.

In the prediction-market context, the customer is not necessarily looking for traditional investing. The user may not want to analyze companies, build a diversified portfolio, or hold long-term securities. The user may want to express a view on a public event.

#### Evidence

- The Week 2 analysis identifies mobile-first financial behavior, digital-payment growth, crypto usage, and investment-platform access as key customer shifts.
- The updated ROI model identifies 651,000 risk-active users as a market proxy.
- The model also uses GEL 6,546 monthly turnover per active user to represent capital cycling through risk-based activity.

#### Strategic implication

This means TBC should not position the product as another brokerage tool. It should position it as a simple, controlled event-contract product for users who want public-event exposure inside a bank-linked wallet.

---

### 3.2 Competition

#### Finding

Competition does not come only from banks or brokerages. It comes from every platform that can capture risk-active attention, wallet balances, payment flows, and trading behavior.

Relevant competitors and substitutes include:

- TBC Capital and Galt & Taggart in brokerage;
- Bank of Georgia and Solo in digital banking;
- Binance, Bybit, Kraken, Werty, GeCrypto, 1tab, and crypto ATMs in crypto access;
- CityPay.io in crypto payment infrastructure;
- Payze and other PSPs in payment rails;
- Adjarabet and other betting operators in risk-active attention;
- Polymarket, Kalshi, and Manifold as global prediction-market benchmarks.

#### Strategic implication

This means TBC should not evaluate the platform only against brokerage. The real competition is for the customer’s wallet, attention, and risk-active behavior. If TBC does not own the platform layer, it risks becoming only a payment rail for someone else’s marketplace.

---

### 3.3 Data

#### Finding

Prediction markets create a new type of data. Market prices reveal aggregate expectations about public events. Trading behavior can also reveal user interest, engagement patterns, liquidity quality, and event demand.

However, this data is sensitive. Trading, KYC, payment, responsible-use, and dispute data cannot be reused casually for marketing. It must be governed for specific platform purposes.

#### Strategic implication

This means data governance is not a back-office issue. It is part of the value proposition. TBC can gain a new market-intelligence layer only if the platform is designed with narrow data purposes, auditability, user protection, and regulator-ready reporting.

---

### 3.4 Innovation

#### Finding

The product cannot follow a launch-first innovation model. Prediction markets carry regulatory, reputational, payment, liquidity, and consumer-protection risks.

The correct innovation model is sandbox-first or special-designation-first. The platform should test markets, wallet flows, responsible-use rules, liquidity, support processes, and outcome resolution before public launch.

#### Strategic implication

This means the project should not be measured by how fast the app can be shipped. It should be measured by how quickly TBC can prove that the model is safe, governable, and financially viable under controlled conditions.

---

### 3.5 Value

#### Finding

Value shifts from traditional asset ownership to event-based exposure. Users are not only buying securities. They are trading the probability of public outcomes.

For TBC, the value is broader than trading revenue. It includes:

- wallet ownership;
- retained balances;
- funding flow;
- payment activity;
- user engagement;
- market intelligence;
- regulated product differentiation;
- first-mover position in Georgian event contracts.

#### Strategic implication

This means the business case is not only “fees from trades.” The stronger TBC case is ownership of the next risk-active wallet layer before foreign platforms, crypto wallets, or betting operators capture it.

## 4. Christensen Disruption Classification

### Classification

The strongest classification is:

> New-market disruption

It is not primarily low-end disruption.

### Why it is not low-end disruption

Low-end disruption occurs when a challenger enters with a cheaper or simpler product for overserved customers. That is not the strongest explanation here.

Low-cost brokerage already exists in Georgia. TBC Capital has already reduced cost barriers through low-minimum and low-cost investing access. Therefore, a prediction-market platform does not mainly win because it is cheaper than brokerage.

### Why it is new-market disruption

Prediction markets create a different job-to-be-done.

Traditional brokerage asks users to:

- understand companies;
- compare securities;
- think about portfolios;
- accept long-term risk;
- use formal investment products.

Prediction markets ask users to:

- understand a public event;
- choose Yes or No;
- accept defined downside;
- see an implied probability;
- trade a short-duration outcome.

This creates access for users who may not participate in traditional brokerage but may understand events such as inflation, exchange rates, NBG decisions, elections, or public-data outcomes.

### Why incumbents may rationally ignore it

Banks and brokerages have rational reasons to avoid prediction markets at first:

1. **Regulatory uncertainty**  
   Event contracts may be viewed as financial instruments, gambling-like products, virtual-asset activity, payment activity, or a new supervised innovation category.

2. **Brand risk**  
   Prediction markets can look similar to betting if the product design is weak. This is uncomfortable for trust-based bank brands.

3. **Business model mismatch**  
   Brokerages are oriented around securities access, AUM, custody, research, and long-term investing. Prediction markets are driven by event turnover, liquidity, and short-term market engagement.

4. **Operational complexity**  
   The platform needs market approval, official-source validation, liquidity support, responsible-use controls, market surveillance, outcome resolution, and dispute handling.

### Updated TBC interpretation

Earlier analysis treated incumbents as firms likely to be disrupted. The updated client assumption changes the conclusion.

If TBC does not act, prediction markets could disrupt parts of future retail engagement by pulling risk-active users toward foreign platforms, crypto wallets, betting operators, or another local bank. But if TBC owns the platform, the same disruption becomes a growth option.

### Strategic implication

This means TBC should not view prediction markets only as a threat to brokerage. It should view them as a new-market opportunity to capture customers who do not want traditional investing but may accept a controlled, bank-backed event-contract product.

## 5. Porter Five Forces

The Porter analysis evaluates industry attractiveness after assuming that an NBG special-designation or sandbox route is plausible. That assumption matters. If regulation is completely blocked, the market is not attractive. If a controlled path exists, the competition shifts to execution: trust, wallet access, liquidity, data sources, responsible use, and settlement.

### Summary table

| Force | Intensity | Score | Named actors | Main evidence | Strategic consequence |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | Medium to high | 4/5 | Banks, fintechs, VASPs, global platforms, betting operators | Technology interface is easy to copy; regulatory comfort, wallet ownership, liquidity, and trust are hard to copy | TBC should move early to define the local model |
| Supplier power | High | 5/5 | NBG, PSPs, banks, liquidity providers, official data sources, cloud/security vendors | Regulators, payment rails, market makers, and source providers control critical inputs | TBC should internalize wallet and settlement where possible |
| Buyer power | Medium | 3/5 | Retail users, active traders, crypto users, betting users, brokerage users | Users can switch easily, but trust, GEL settlement, and official-source resolution reduce friction | Product must be simple, transparent, and trusted |
| Threat of substitutes | Very high | 5/5 | Adjarabet, crypto exchanges, TBC Capital, Galt & Taggart, real estate speculation, cash savings | Risk-active users already have alternatives | Prediction markets must be more controlled than betting and simpler than brokerage |
| Rivalry among existing competitors | Medium | 3/5 | TBC, Bank of Georgia, Binance, Bybit, CityPay, Payze, betting operators | No regulated local prediction-market rival yet, but adjacent platforms already own user attention and payment flows | First mover can shape the category, but only with strong execution |

---

### 5.1 Threat of new entrants

#### Intensity: Medium to high, 4/5

#### Named potential entrants

- Bank of Georgia or Galt & Taggart
- Pave Bank
- Binance or Bybit
- CityPay.io
- Payze or other payment gateways
- Betting operators such as Adjarabet
- International prediction-market platforms such as Polymarket or Kalshi
- Local fintech startups

#### Rationale

The user interface is not a major barrier. A Yes/No market page can be built quickly. The difficult parts are:

- NBG classification;
- bank-grade KYC;
- GEL settlement;
- wallet funding and reconciliation;
- official-source resolution;
- liquidity and market-maker support;
- responsible-use controls;
- dispute handling;
- regulator and payment-partner reporting.

#### Strategic implication

This means first-mover advantage is not enough. TBC should move early, but it must also own the hard-to-copy layers: wallet, trust, settlement, regulatory governance, and liquidity.

---

### 5.2 Supplier power

#### Intensity: High, 5/5

#### Key suppliers and gatekeepers

- NBG
- Payment service providers
- Bank rails and wallet infrastructure
- Liquidity providers and market makers
- Official data sources such as NBG, Geostat, and CEC
- Cloud, cybersecurity, and identity-verification vendors
- Legal and compliance advisers

#### Rationale

Suppliers control whether the platform can operate at scale. NBG influences classification and sandbox acceptance. PSPs and banks influence payment economics. Market makers influence spreads and market quality. Official data sources influence resolution trust.

The Week 2 industry analysis notes that digital-payment infrastructure and VASP regulation are important foundations, but prediction markets remain undefined. This means supplier and gatekeeper power remains high.

#### Strategic implication

This means TBC should reduce supplier dependency where possible. The most important step is to use a TBC-linked GEL wallet and bank-controlled settlement architecture rather than relying mainly on external PSP or crypto rails.

---

### 5.3 Buyer power

#### Intensity: Medium, 3/5

#### Named buyer groups

- risk-active retail users;
- crypto users;
- betting users;
- entry-level investors;
- news-driven speculators;
- mobile banking users.

#### Rationale

Users have many alternatives and low switching costs. They can choose betting apps, crypto platforms, brokerage apps, cash savings, or no product at all. However, buyer power is reduced if the product offers:

- trusted TBC wallet access;
- official-source outcomes;
- transparent market rules;
- clear settlement;
- responsible-use protections;
- easy mobile banking access.

#### Strategic implication

This means TBC must compete on trust and simplicity, not only price. A zero-fee interface is not enough. Users must believe that markets are fair, outcomes are clear, and funds are safe.

---

### 5.4 Threat of substitutes

#### Intensity: Very high, 5/5

#### Named substitutes

- Adjarabet and betting operators;
- Binance, Bybit, Kraken, Werty, GeCrypto, 1tab, and Cryptomat;
- TBC Capital and Galt & Taggart brokerage;
- real estate speculation;
- cash savings and bank deposits;
- foreign prediction-market platforms.

#### Rationale

Prediction markets compete for time, attention, risk appetite, and wallet balances. Many substitutes already exist. Betting platforms are behaviorally close. Crypto platforms are digitally close. Brokerage products are financially legitimate but behaviorally different.

#### Strategic implication

This means the platform must occupy a clear position:

> simpler than brokerage, more controlled than betting, more locally trusted than crypto, and more bank-integrated than foreign prediction-market platforms.

---

### 5.5 Rivalry among existing competitors

#### Intensity: Medium, 3/5

#### Named competitors

- TBC Bank / Space / TBC Capital
- Bank of Georgia / Solo / Galt & Taggart
- Binance and Bybit
- CityPay.io
- Payze
- Adjarabet and betting operators
- Pave Bank

#### Rationale

There is no regulated Georgian prediction-market platform yet. That lowers direct rivalry. However, adjacent rivalry is already strong because multiple players own parts of the value chain:

- banks own trust and customer relationships;
- crypto platforms own speculative liquidity;
- betting platforms own risk-active attention;
- PSPs own payment integration;
- brokerages own regulated investment access.

#### Strategic implication

This means TBC’s window is time-sensitive. If another actor combines wallet, event exposure, and regulatory positioning first, TBC may lose the category even if it still processes payments.

## 6. Competitive Landscape

### 6.1 Competitive positioning

The proposed platform should be placed in the **digital-native and regulated** quadrant.

This is the strongest quadrant because it combines:

- mobile-first user experience;
- bank trust;
- NBG engagement;
- GEL settlement;
- KYC and AML controls;
- responsible-use rules;
- official-source resolution;
- regulator-ready reporting.

### 6.2 Main competitor groups

| Competitor group | Examples | What they own | What they lack | Threat to TBC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Georgian banks and brokerages | Bank of Georgia, Galt & Taggart, TBC Capital | Trust, regulation, customer base, payments | Event-contract product, prediction-market liquidity | High if they move first |
| Crypto and VASP platforms | Binance, Bybit, Kraken, Werty, GeCrypto, 1tab | Speculative users, wallets, liquidity, trading culture | Bank-grade trust, GEL-native local governance, consumer-protection framing | High |
| Payment infrastructure players | Payze, CityPay.io, AzRy | Payment integration, merchant rails, technical infrastructure | Consumer prediction-market brand, bank trust, market governance | Medium |
| Betting operators | Adjarabet and other gambling platforms | Risk-active attention, high-frequency behavior, entertainment positioning | Bank legitimacy, financial-market credibility, responsible-use trust | High as substitutes |
| Global prediction-market platforms | Polymarket, Kalshi, Manifold | Category proof, product know-how, market design | Georgian regulatory classification, local wallet ownership, GEL settlement, TBC customer relationship | Medium to high |
| TBC-backed platform | Proposed model | Trust, wallet, KYC, mobile app, payment rails, local governance | Must build event-contract operations and liquidity | Strongest target position |

### 6.3 Why TBC should not simply partner with Polymarket or Kalshi

International prediction-market platforms are important benchmarks, but they should not be the ownership model.

A simple partnership could create four problems:

1. **Wallet leakage**  
   User balances may move into an external wallet or platform-controlled account.

2. **Customer relationship loss**  
   The international platform may own the product experience, user behavior, and engagement loop.

3. **Data control weakness**  
   TBC may not fully control market, behavior, risk, and wallet data.

4. **Regulatory responsibility without platform economics**  
   TBC could carry payment, brand, or compliance exposure while the platform owner captures economics.

### Strategic implication

TBC should partner for knowledge, liquidity, or technical support only if it keeps ownership of the Georgian wallet layer, customer relationship, KYC, GEL settlement, and regulator-facing governance model.

## 7. Key Competitive Dynamics

### Dynamic 1: Wallet ownership is becoming the strategic control point

#### Finding

The platform that owns the wallet owns the funding flow, settlement flow, user identity layer, and a large part of the customer relationship.

#### Strategic consequence

TBC should not treat the prediction-market platform as a referral product. It should integrate it into a TBC-linked wallet or mobile banking environment, with bank-controlled funding, withdrawal, KYC, monitoring, and support.

---

### Dynamic 2: Global platforms prove the category, but local control determines viability

#### Finding

Polymarket, Kalshi, and Manifold show that prediction markets can attract users, create liquidity, and turn public events into tradable probabilities. However, their models are not directly transferable to Georgia.

#### Strategic consequence

The Georgian model should be locally controlled. It should use global platforms as benchmarks, not as ownership templates. The target model is TBC-controlled, GEL-settled, and regulator-facing.

---

### Dynamic 3: Betting platforms own attention, but banks own trust

#### Finding

Betting operators are close behavioral substitutes because they already capture risk-active attention and high-frequency event behavior. However, they do not provide bank-grade financial legitimacy.

#### Strategic consequence

TBC should avoid betting-style positioning. The platform should be framed as a regulated event-contract marketplace with official sources, defined downside, responsible-use limits, and transparent resolution.

---

### Dynamic 4: Crypto platforms own speculative liquidity, but not mainstream bank trust

#### Finding

Crypto platforms offer speed, liquidity, wallets, and trading culture. However, they may lack mainstream trust, GEL-native settlement, and local regulator-facing governance.

#### Strategic consequence

A crypto or VASP route should remain a fallback or niche option, not the mass-market base case. TBC’s strongest advantage is to make the product bank-controlled and GEL-settled.

---

### Dynamic 5: Brokerage is legitimate, but does not solve the customer job

#### Finding

TBC Capital and Galt & Taggart provide formal investment access. However, brokerage products are designed around securities, portfolios, and longer-term investing. Prediction markets serve a different job: simple, short-term event exposure.

#### Strategic consequence

The platform should not be positioned as a cheaper brokerage product. It should be positioned as a different product category that captures users who understand events better than securities.

---

### Dynamic 6: Liquidity is the cold-start constraint

#### Finding

Prediction markets require active users and liquidity providers at the same time. Without liquidity, spreads are wide and users do not trust the market. Without users, market makers do not have enough reason to provide liquidity.

#### Strategic consequence

TBC should fund a controlled liquidity and market-maker programme before public scale. Market quality should be a launch condition, not a post-launch optimization.

---

### Dynamic 7: Regulation is now an opening, not only a barrier

#### Finding

Earlier analysis treated regulatory uncertainty mainly as a constraint. The updated strategy assumes that NBG may be open to exploring a special designation or sandbox route for a controlled event-contract platform.

#### Strategic consequence

The question shifts from “can prediction markets exist?” to “who can design the Georgian model responsibly first?” This strengthens the case for TBC because it has the trust, compliance resources, and regulator-facing credibility to shape the category.

---

## 8. Strategic Consequences for TBC

### Consequence 1: TBC should own the category, not outsource it

TBC should not rely on a foreign platform as the primary owner of the product. Partnerships may help with technology, liquidity, or know-how, but TBC should retain the wallet, customer relationship, KYC layer, GEL settlement, and regulator-facing governance.

### Consequence 2: The platform should be inside or adjacent to mobile banking

The product should not be a disconnected speculative app. The strategic value comes from integration with TBC’s digital ecosystem, where user identity, wallet funding, responsible-use controls, and customer support can be governed consistently.

### Consequence 3: The base case should remain GEL-settled

Crypto-native settlement may be useful as a fallback, but it should not be the core mass-market model. GEL settlement supports local trust, regulatory comfort, payment clarity, and wallet ownership.

### Consequence 4: The product must avoid gambling positioning

The platform should not target vulnerable users, gambling-excluded users, or sports-betting demand as the base case. The safer initial market set should focus on official-source public events such as inflation, exchange-rate ranges, NBG policy decisions, and election turnout.

### Consequence 5: Public launch should depend on proof, not enthusiasm

The platform should scale only after the sandbox validates:

- user adoption;
- turnover velocity;
- wallet funding behavior;
- payment cost;
- liquidity quality;
- dispute rate;
- responsible-use controls;
- regulator comfort;
- contribution margin.

## 9. Board-Level Conclusion

The industry is attractive because Georgia already has risk-active behavior, strong digital banking, growing payment infrastructure, and no regulated local prediction-market category.

The industry is difficult because the platform must solve regulation, wallet ownership, liquidity, responsible use, official-source resolution, market surveillance, and user trust at the same time.

This makes the TBC-backed model the most defensible route.

A standalone fintech would struggle with trust, CAC, payments, and regulation. A crypto-native route would struggle with mainstream acceptance and event-contract legality. A foreign-platform partnership could weaken TBC’s control over the wallet and customer relationship.

The recommended competitive position is therefore:

> TBC should build the first regulated Georgian event-contract marketplace inside a TBC-controlled wallet and mobile banking environment, using NBG designation, GEL settlement, official-source resolution, responsible-use controls, and liquidity support as the core strategic advantages.

For the final presentation, this section should support four slide-level conclusions:

1. **Georgia has a real category gap:** risk-active behavior exists, but no regulated local event-contract platform owns it.
