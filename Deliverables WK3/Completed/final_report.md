## 1. Executive Summary

This report recommends that TBC approve an 18-month controlled launch path for a TBC-owned, GEL-settled prediction-market platform integrated into or adjacent to the TBC mobile banking and wallet ecosystem. The product should launch through an NBG special-designation or sandbox-first route, with responsible-use controls, official-source resolution, market surveillance, mobile banking integration, and regulator-ready governance built before public scale.

The three most important conclusions are clear. First, Georgia has a real market gap for simple, regulated event-contract exposure. Current alternatives include betting, crypto trading, brokerage, cash savings, and foreign platforms, but none combine event-based exposure with Georgian bank trust, GEL settlement, official-source resolution, and bank-grade user protection. Second, regulation is the unlock, but ownership is the strategic prize. TBC should not allow a foreign platform, crypto venue, betting operator, or competing bank to capture the wallet, customer relationship, and event-market data layer first. Third, the financial case is attractive only if TBC owns the wallet-to-market loop. The base case assumes 65,100 active users, GEL 6,546 monthly turnover per active user, a 1 percent take rate, GEL 27.95M annual benefit at stabilization, 8.9-month payback, 99.9 percent IRR, and GEL 57.99M 5-year NPV at an 18 percent discount rate.

TBC should fund this rather than simply partner with Polymarket, Kalshi, or another provider because partnership may import know-how but does not solve the Georgian control problem. NBG classification, GEL settlement, KYC, wallet funding, responsible-use interventions, dispute handling, Georgian-language disclosures, data protection, and regulator-ready evidence still need local ownership. A partner could support liquidity or technology, but TBC should keep the wallet, mobile banking access point, customer relationship, market data, and governance layer.

The cost of inaction is strategic. If a foreign or crypto-native platform enters first, TBC may remain only a payment rail while another actor owns customer attention, wallet behavior, market data, and pricing infrastructure. If a betting operator frames the category first, prediction markets may be pulled toward gambling positioning. If another bank moves first, TBC loses the opportunity to define the trusted local model.

The required board decision is not an immediate public launch. It is approval to fund H1 foundation and H2 sandbox validation, including the Year 1 capital requirement of GEL 12.00M, split into GEL 7.40M CapEx and GEL 4.60M OpEx. Public launch should be conditional on regulatory comfort, wallet reliability, liquidity quality, responsible-use evidence, dispute control, payment economics, and unit economics that track closer to the base case than the severe downside case.

### Strategic implication

The platform should be treated as a controlled marketplace, not a normal mobile app feature and not a betting-style product. TBC should proceed only if it can prove that wallet funding, market approval, user protection, surveillance, resolution, and governance work together before scale.

## 2. Industry and Competitive Analysis

### 2.1 Industry Definition

The relevant industry is not conventional brokerage alone. It is the emerging intersection of mobile banking, digital wallets, event-contract markets, payment monitoring, crypto and VASP activity, betting substitutes, trading interfaces, and regulatory control systems. The product is not cheap brokerage. It is new-market creation around simple, event-based exposure.

### 2.2 Rogers' Five Domains

| Domain | Evidence | Strategic consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | Risk-active users already engage with betting, crypto, brokerage, and speculative assets. Mobile banking users also expect low-friction financial experiences. | The platform must be simple enough for non-brokerage users but controlled enough for a bank context. |
| Competition | Adjacent competitors include TBC Capital, Bank of Georgia/Galt & Taggart, Binance, Bybit, CityPay, Payze, Adjarabet-style operators, Polymarket, Kalshi, and Manifold. | TBC should compete through trust, GEL settlement, wallet ownership, and official-source resolution. |
| Data | Market outcomes depend on official sources such as NBG, Geostat, CEC, and FX reference data. User-risk controls depend on wallet, trading, and support data. | Data governance and official-source registries must be built before AI deployment and public scale. |
| Innovation | The product carries regulatory, reputational, payment, liquidity, and consumer-protection risk. | Innovation must be sandbox-first, with stage gates rather than launch-first scaling. |
| Value | Users receive simple exposure to public outcomes. TBC receives wallet engagement, retained balances, fee revenue, and market data. Regulators receive a controlled alternative to offshore or gambling-like substitutes. | TBC should own the category rather than outsource the highest-value layers. |

### 2.3 Christensen Disruption Classification

The recommended platform is best classified as new-market disruption, not low-end disruption. It does not simply offer brokerage at lower cost. It creates a simpler category for users who may not want full securities investing but do want structured exposure to public events such as inflation, GEL exchange-rate bands, NBG decisions, election turnout, or official macroeconomic releases.

Incumbents may rationally ignore the category because early volumes are uncertain, classification is ambiguous, liquidity must be subsidized, and reputational risk is high. That is why the correct structure is a ring-fenced sandbox-first platform, not an immediate mass-market product.

### 2.4 Porter Five Forces

| Force | Intensity | Score | Named actors | Strategic consequence |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | Medium to high | 4/5 | Banks, fintechs, VASPs, global platforms, betting operators | TBC should move early to define the local model. |
| Supplier power | High | 5/5 | NBG, PSPs, banks, liquidity providers, official data sources, cloud/security vendors | TBC should internalize wallet and settlement where possible. |

### 2.5 Key Competitive Dynamics

Wallet ownership is becoming the strategic control point. If users fund foreign platforms, external crypto wallets, or betting accounts, TBC loses balances, payment flow, customer engagement, and behavioral insight. Global platforms prove demand, but local control determines viability. Betting platforms own attention, but banks own trust. Crypto platforms own speculative liquidity, but not mainstream Georgian bank trust. Brokerage is legitimate, but it does not solve the same customer job.

### Conclusion

The category is attractive only if TBC controls the hard-to-copy layers: wallet, GEL settlement, mobile banking access, official-source resolution, responsible-use controls, market surveillance, and regulator-facing governance.

### 3.3 Scoring Rationale

The highest-scoring use cases either protect user funds, protect users from harm, protect market integrity, or produce evidence for regulators. Responsible-use scoring and wallet-risk monitoring score 24 because they are essential to the mobile journey and regulatory credibility. Market explanations score 22 because poor wording can cause misunderstanding and complaints. Event eligibility and outcome resolution score 21 because no market should exist without an approved category, official source, payout rule, and dispute path.

### 3.4 Benchmark Gap Analysis

Kalshi is the most relevant regulated benchmark because it shows the discipline required for event-contract markets: category rules, regulatory engagement, market listing controls, surveillance, and resolution procedures. Polymarket is a useful product and liquidity benchmark because it shows user demand, market interface simplicity, and community-driven market activity. The gap for TBC is not model sophistication. The gap is controlled data, mobile-app integration, human override, and regulator-ready evidence.

### Strategic implication

AI should be embedded as control infrastructure. It should screen, draft, flag, route, summarize, and report. It should not approve markets, freeze accounts, resolve payouts, impose long restrictions, or submit regulator-facing reports without human review.

## 4. Platform Strategy Analysis

### 4.1 Platform Competitive Structure

The platform should be designed as a wallet-to-market loop. Users discover markets through TBC mobile banking or an adjacent controlled entry point, fund a TBC-linked GEL wallet, trade official-source event contracts, receive resolution and settlement inside the same ecosystem, and access support or dispute routes through Customer Trust.

### 4.2 Network Effects

Prediction markets have liquidity-driven network effects. More users improve order depth, tighter spreads, and market quality. Better market quality improves trust and repeat usage. However, unmanaged network effects can also amplify manipulation, impulsive trading, and dispute volume. Therefore, growth must be bounded by responsible-use controls, surveillance, official-source resolution, and liquidity monitoring.

### 4.3 Client's Current Platform Position

TBC already owns strong platform assets: mobile banking distribution, customer trust, KYC capabilities, payment and wallet infrastructure, support channels, data governance experience, and regulator relationships. These assets make TBC better positioned than a standalone fintech to create a Georgian event-contract category. The weakness is that TBC does not currently operate a supervised event-contract marketplace, so governance, market operations, liquidity support, and resolution capabilities must be built.

### 4.4 Threat Assessment

| Threat | Named entrants or substitutes | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign prediction-market entry | Polymarket, Kalshi-style provider, Manifold-style product | Foreign platform captures the user relationship and market behavior data. |
| Crypto-native route | Binance, Bybit, VASP operators, crypto wallets | TBC becomes a funding or cash-out rail rather than the platform owner. |
| Betting platform reframing | Adjarabet-style operators | Category becomes gambling-positioned, increasing reputational and regulatory risk. |
| Bank competitor entry | Bank of Georgia, Galt & Taggart, other financial groups | TBC loses first-mover advantage in regulated local event contracts. |
| PSP-led platform | CityPay, Payze, payment gateways | Payment layer captures transaction flow without bank-grade market governance. |

### 4.5 Mobile Banking Integration

Mobile banking integration is a core platform component, not a channel detail. The product should have its own controlled entry point, eligibility gate, product education screen, risk quiz or confirmation, daily exposure and loss-limit display, wallet funding flow, order confirmation showing maximum possible loss, support and dispute route, and audit log for user-impacting actions.

### Conclusion

The main platform threat is a foreign or crypto-native platform capturing the wallet and customer relationship while TBC becomes only a payment rail. The strategic answer is for TBC to own the wallet-to-market loop before competitors define the category.

## 5. Strategic Options Evaluation

### 5.1 Options

| Option | Description | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Option 1: TBC-owned GEL wallet platform | TBC owns the wallet, mobile banking access, customer relationship, GEL settlement, market approval, governance, and reporting under an NBG route. | Highest execution complexity. |
| Option 2: International platform partnership | TBC partners with or white-labels a Polymarket/Kalshi-style provider for technology, market design, or liquidity support. | Strategic dependency and reduced control over wallet, data, economics, and governance. |
| Option 3: Crypto/VASP-first standalone route | Platform launches through crypto rails, USDC-style settlement, or VASP infrastructure with weaker bank integration. | Regulatory ambiguity, weaker mainstream trust, and reduced GEL wallet ownership. |

### 5.2 Four-Criterion Evaluation Matrix

| Option | Strategic control | Regulatory and trust fit | Financial attractiveness | Execution feasibility | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Option 1: TBC-owned GEL wallet platform | 5 | 5 | 5 | 4 | 19/20 |
| Option 2: International platform partnership | 3 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 13/20 |
| Option 3: Crypto/VASP-first standalone platform | 2 | 2 | 3 | 3 | 10/20 |

### 5.3 Recommended Option

The recommended option is Option 1: a TBC-owned GEL wallet platform under an NBG special-designation or sandbox-first route. It is recommended for three reasons. It best solves the wallet ownership problem. It has the strongest regulatory and trust fit. It captures the strongest financial upside because bank distribution can lower CAC, GEL wallet settlement can reduce payment friction, and TBC trust can support adoption.

### Conclusion

TBC may use partners for technology, liquidity, or benchmarking, but it should not outsource ownership of the Georgian wallet and governance layer.

## 6. Recommended Business Model

### 6.1 Value Proposition

For users, the product offers simple exposure to public outcomes inside a trusted bank-linked environment. For TBC, it creates wallet engagement, retained balances, customer insight, trading-related revenue, and a first-mover position in regulated Georgian event contracts. For regulators, it creates a controlled alternative to offshore, crypto-native, or gambling-like substitutes. For payment partners and market makers, it creates a supervised environment with KYC, AML, audit trails, and market-quality monitoring.

### 6.2 Operating Model Changes by Function

| Function | Required change |
|---|---|
| Mobile Banking Product | Move from channel delivery to controlled product distribution with eligibility, warnings, wallet funding, exposure displays, and support routing. |
| Product and Engineering | Build controlled workflows, not only screens: source validation, audit logs, wallet flows, user notices, and override logs. |
| Payments, KYC, AML, and Wallet Operations | Add platform-specific wallet controls, funding velocity checks, withdrawal holds, duplicate-identity review, and settlement reconciliation. |
| Market Operations | Approve markets through official sources, payout rules, risk disclosures, and dispute paths before listing. |
| Compliance and Legal | Become embedded decision owners for market taxonomy, risk warnings, legal classification, and NBG engagement. |
| Responsible Use | Own limits, alerts, cooling-off periods, appeal routes, and intervention thresholds. |
| Customer Trust | Handle payout disputes, payment holds, account restrictions, wallet questions, and responsible-use appeals with bilingual scripts. |
| Data and AI | Maintain source registry, model register, audit logs, surveillance alerts, app-interaction logs, and governed wallet-event data. |
| Governance and Board Oversight | Use stage gates based on evidence, not technical completion alone. |

### 6.3 Revenue Model Logic

The core formula is:

```text
Active traders x monthly turnover per active user x take rate = monthly gross revenue
```

The base case is:

```text
65,100 active users x GEL 6,546 monthly turnover x 1% take rate = GEL 4.26M monthly gross revenue
```

The primary revenue stream is spread capture or maker/taker trading fee. Future optional revenue streams may include compliant market-creation fees, analytics products, premium features, or B2B products, but these should not be treated as base-case revenue before liquidity is proven.

### 6.4 Data and AI Infrastructure Requirements

The platform requires an official-source registry, market taxonomy, wallet ledger, KYC and AML records, trading and order logs, responsible-use logs, support and dispute records, resolution evidence archives, AI model register, human override log, and regulator-ready reporting layer. Data use should be narrow and auditable. Trading, payment, support, and responsible-use data should not quietly become marketing data.

### Conclusion

The business model works only if TBC treats the platform as a controlled marketplace. Mobile banking distribution is valuable, but it must be paired with official-source resolution, responsible-use controls, market surveillance, GEL settlement, and regulator-ready reporting.

## 11. Governance Plan

### 11.1 Oversight Body

The oversight body should be the Prediction Market Governance and AI Risk Committee. It should include the Prediction Market General Manager, Compliance Lead or MLRO, Legal Counsel, Head of Data and AI, Market Operations Lead, Market Integrity Lead, Responsible Use Lead, Payments/KYC/Settlement Lead, Customer Trust Lead, Information Security Lead, bank sponsor representative, Internal Audit observer, and Board Risk Committee representative for launch gates.

### 11.2 Decision Rights and Cadence

The committee has final decision rights over market categories, event-taxonomy exceptions, AI model deployment and rollback, responsible-use thresholds, extended market pauses, severe market-integrity cases, disputed outcomes above threshold, regulator-facing incident reports, payment-partner reporting packs, public launch recommendations, and mobile-app integration decisions. It meets monthly in months 1 to 3, every two weeks in months 4 to 6, every two weeks during sandbox, and within 24 hours for severe incidents.

### 11.3 Escalation

Legal classification, NBG engagement, and gambling-risk concerns escalate to the Chief Compliance Officer, external counsel, and Board Risk Committee. Payment, wallet, and AML issues escalate to the Chief Risk Officer delegate and bank payments risk owner. User-harm concerns escalate to the General Manager and Board Risk representative. Model failure, drift, or incomplete logs escalate to Head of Data and AI, Information Security, and Internal Audit. If unresolved issues affect legality, payout accuracy, user funds, account restrictions, or regulator communication, the default action is to pause the workflow.

### 11.4 Mobile Banking App Integration Decision Rights

The committee should approve product placement, naming, onboarding, risk quiz language, exposure and loss-limit displays, order confirmation wording, responsible-use alerts, cooling-off communication, wallet funding and withdrawal flow, support escalation path, app analytics, and criteria for expanding access beyond sandbox users. No mobile-app release should go live unless the customer journey, risk warnings, wallet flow, support routing, and audit-log requirements are approved.

### 11.5 EU AI Act-Style Risk Classification

| AI use case | Risk tier | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Event eligibility screening | Limited if advisory; high if automatic approval | Market eligibility affects regulatory exposure and user access. |
| Contract drafting and risk disclosure | Limited if reviewed; high if published without review | User-facing text can mislead users. |
| Market surveillance | High | Alerts can lead to review, market pause, holds, or escalation. |
| Responsible-use scoring | High | Scores can trigger warnings, limits, cooling-off, or restrictions. |
| Payment, KYC, and fraud monitoring | High | System can delay onboarding, deposits, withdrawals, or wallet access. |
| Outcome-resolution support | High | Result extraction can affect payout timing and trust. |
| Dispute triage | Limited if routing only; high if rejecting disputes | Automatic rejection would affect user rights. |
| Liquidity monitoring | Limited if monitoring only; high if automatic pause | Automatic market action affects user-facing status. |
| Reporting automation | Limited if drafting only; high if submitted without sign-off | External reports require human accountability. |

### 11.7 Georgian Data Protection Obligations

The platform should apply purpose limitation, data minimization, privacy by design and default, security of processing, transparency and user rights, and auditability. Trading, wallet, KYC, support, dispute, responsible-use, and device data should be used only for defined platform purposes. User-impacting automated support should always include a route to human review.

### Conclusion

Governance is the operating license of the platform. AI may support decisions, but humans must own market approval, user restrictions, payout resolution, and regulator-facing communication.

### 12.1 H1 Use Case Reflection

| H1 AI use case | Bias or fairness risk | Transparency obligation | Accountability structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Event eligibility screening | Category bias may over-accept globally popular markets or over-reject Georgian civic events. | Market page must disclose official source, category, resolution rule, warning, and dispute path. | Market Operations prepares; Compliance reviews; Governance Committee approves listing. |
| Contract drafting and risk disclosure | Comprehension inequality between expert and entry-level users. | User-facing wording must explain event, source, timing, maximum loss, and dispute rights. | Market Operations owns wording; Compliance owns warning; Resolution Committee owns payout clarity. |
| Responsible-use framework and intervention routing | Over-protection may restrict healthy users; under-protection may miss harmful patterns. | Users must know what actions can trigger alerts, cooling-off, restrictions, or review. | Responsible Use Lead owns interventions; Compliance reviews thresholds; appeals are human-reviewed. |
| Payment, KYC, wallet, and fraud monitoring | False positives may affect users with shared devices or irregular payment patterns. | Users must be informed when onboarding, deposits, withdrawals, or wallet access are delayed for review. | Payments/KYC lead owns wallet actions; AML cases escalate to Compliance. |
| Market surveillance prototype | Manipulation models may flag active users or miss coordinated behavior. | Full surveillance logic cannot be disclosed, but market pauses or account reviews need meaningful notice where user-impacting. | Market Integrity Lead investigates; Compliance escalates severe cases. |
| Dispute triage and support routing | Automatic routing may deprioritize users with less formal language or incomplete evidence. | Users must have clear support, dispute, and appeal channels in Georgian and English. | Customer Trust Lead owns routing quality and escalation. |
| Reporting readiness and governance evidence | Report summarization may understate disputes, complaints, or intervention frequency. | Internal reviewers must know which parts were AI-generated; external reports require human sign-off. | Regulatory Governance Lead prepares; Compliance signs off. |
| Mobile banking app integration controls | Suitability prompts may over-target or under-inform users based on app behavior. | App onboarding must clearly explain risk, limits, funding, maximum loss, support, and cooling-off. | Product Lead owns journey; Compliance and Responsible Use approve wording and thresholds. |

### 12.2 Distinction from Governance

Governance defines authority, thresholds, escalation, and audit requirements. Responsible AI defines fairness, transparency, and accountability to affected individuals. Both are required before sandbox users trade.

### Conclusion

The responsible AI standard is practical: every high-impact AI output must be understandable, contestable, documented, and owned by a human role.
